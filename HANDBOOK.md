## 1. Purpose of the App

This app is designed as a complete teaching companion for the course **Storytelling using Data Visualization**. It helps the instructor deliver concepts, examples, visual demonstrations, activities, quizzes, business cases, role-play exercises, and final workshop tasks from one place.

The teaching emphasis is not limited to making charts. The app repeatedly trains students to move through the full decision-story chain:

```text
Business Context → Data Evidence → Visual Pattern → Insight → Implication → Recommendation → Action
```

The app should be used as a live classroom system. It is suitable for projection, discussion, group work, quick formative checks, and end-term preparation.

## 3. Recommended 75-Minute Class Flow

Use the following rhythm for most sessions:

| Time | Segment | Instructor Action | Expected Student Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–5 min | Opening hook | Start with a managerial situation or visual problem. | One quick answer or reaction. |
| 5–15 min | Theoratical Concepts | Explain the key concept using the app’s instructor notes. | Identify the decision question. |
| 15–30 min | Visual demonstration | Show the chart, diagram, or interactive demo. | Observation plus evidence. |
| 30–50 min | Guided activity | Conduct the mini-lab or activity bank task. | Short chart critique, storyboard, or recommendation. |
| 50–62 min | Peer discussion | Students compare answers and improve one sentence. | Improved visual insight. |
| 62–70 min | Quiz and misconception check | Use reveal-answer quiz questions. | Reasoned answer and explanation. |
| 70–75 min | Closing reflection | Ask for one action-oriented takeaway. | Manager-ready recommendation. |

8. **Quiz** — check understanding.
9. **Reflection** — close with a managerial takeaway.

### Step 3: Use Tell Me the Story

The **Tell me the story** button is meant to help the instructor explain each concept through a classroom-friendly narrative. Use it when students need an intuitive explanation before technical terms.

### Step 4: Reveal Solutions Gradually

Do not show model solutions immediately. First ask students:

1. What do you observe?
2. What evidence supports it?
3. What could be misunderstood?
4. What should management do?
5. What limitation should be mentioned?

Then reveal the model solution.

## 5. Session-Wise Teaching Guide

### Session 1: Why Data Visualization and Storytelling Matter

**Teaching objective:** Help students understand that visuals are not decoration; they are tools for managerial attention and decision-making.

**Opening example:** A leadership team sees ten tables of numbers and discusses five different issues. A single ranked chart focuses attention on the real priority.

**Right way:** Start with the decision question, show one clear visual, and connect it to action.

**Wrong way:** Show tables or decorative visuals without explaining what management should conclude.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may think a chart automatically creates insight.

**Clarification:** A chart supports insight, but the presenter must still explain business meaning.

**Activity:** Ask students to convert a table into one ranked visual and write a one-sentence managerial takeaway.

**Model answer:** “West and South are driving sales, while Central needs diagnosis before the next budget allocation.”

---

### Session 2: Mapping Data to Visual Forms

**Teaching objective:** Train students to select charts based on the analytical question.

**Core rule:**

| Question Type | Suitable Visual |
|---|---|
| Compare/rank | Bar chart or dot plot |
| Show trend | Line chart |
| Show relationship | Scatter plot |
| Show distribution | Histogram or box plot |
| Show composition | Stacked bar or 100% stacked bar |

**Right way:** Choose the chart after identifying the business question.

**Wrong way:** Choose the chart because it looks attractive.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may believe one chart type is always best.

**Clarification:** The best chart depends on question, data type, and audience.

**Activity:** Give students five managerial questions and ask them to select the best visual.

---

### Session 3: Coordinate Systems, Axes, and Scales

**Teaching objective:** Show how scale, axes, and framing can change interpretation.

**Opening example:** A small satisfaction gap appears dramatic when the axis is truncated.

**Right way:** Use fair scale choices and clearly explain axis decisions.

**Wrong way:** Use compressed scales to exaggerate differences.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may argue that the chart is fine because the numbers are correct.

**Clarification:** A technically correct chart can still be misleading if it visually exaggerates the situation.

**Activity:** Redesign a misleading axis and write the corrected interpretation.

---

### Session 4: Color, Emphasis, and Visual Attention

**Teaching objective:** Teach students to use color as attention guidance, not decoration.

**Right way:** Use neutral colors for context and one accent color for the key message.

**Wrong way:** Use too many bright colors with no hierarchy.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may think more colors make the chart more professional.

**Clarification:** Color should encode meaning or guide attention.

**Activity:** Redesign a multi-color chart using one highlight color and a clearer title.

---

### Session 5: Visualizing Amounts with a Message

**Teaching objective:** Teach comparison visuals such as ranked bar charts, dot plots, and heatmaps.

**Right way:** Sort values, use meaningful labels, and write an insight-led title.

**Wrong way:** Use unsorted categories and generic titles such as “Sales by Region.”

**Model title:** “West leads revenue, while Central requires immediate recovery attention.”

**Activity:** Convert a descriptive title into an action-oriented title.

---

### Session 6: Visualizing Distributions and Variation

**Teaching objective:** Help students look beyond averages.

**Key message:** Averages hide spread, skew, outliers, and risk.

**Right way:** Use histograms or box plots to show variation.

**Wrong way:** Compare only averages and ignore consistency.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may assume equal averages mean equal performance.

**Clarification:** Two teams can have the same average but very different risk profiles.

---

### Session 7: Visualizing Proportions and Composition

**Teaching objective:** Explain part-to-whole storytelling.

**Right way:** Use composition charts when contribution, mix, or dependency is the question.

**Wrong way:** Use pie charts with too many slices or when exact ranking is required.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may think pie charts are always wrong.

**Clarification:** Pie charts can work for a small number of categories, but sorted bars are often clearer.

---

### Session 8: Critiquing and Reframing Weak Visual Stories

**Teaching objective:** Train students to critique visuals constructively.

**Critique checklist:**

1. What is the decision question?
2. Is the chart truthful?
3. Is the message clear?
4. Is the design uncluttered?
5. Is the recommendation justified?

**Right way:** Critique truthfulness, clarity, and usefulness.

**Wrong way:** Critique only aesthetics.

---

### Session 9: Visualizing Relationships and Building Insight

**Teaching objective:** Teach association, clusters, and outliers using scatter plots.

**Right way:** Use scatter plots to generate hypotheses and identify patterns.

**Wrong way:** Claim causation from correlation.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may write “X causes Y” after seeing a scatter plot.

**Clarification:** A scatter plot can suggest association; causality needs stronger evidence.

---

### Session 10: Visualizing Time Series and Change Over Time

**Teaching objective:** Teach trend, seasonality, turning points, and event annotation.

**Right way:** Narrate what changed, when it changed, whether the change sustained, and what might explain it.

**Wrong way:** Claim causality immediately after an event marker.

**Misinterpretation:** “Revenue increased after the campaign, so the campaign caused it.”

**Clarification:** Sequence supports a hypothesis, not proof.

---

### Session 11: Dashboard Storytelling for Business Audiences

**Teaching objective:** Teach dashboard hierarchy and executive communication.

**Ideal dashboard logic:**

```text
KPI Summary → Diagnostic Chart → Supporting Detail → Action Note
```

**Right way:** Build dashboards around a decision question.

**Wrong way:** Add many unrelated charts.

**Misinterpretation:** Students may believe more charts mean better dashboards.

**Clarification:** A strong dashboard reduces cognitive load.

---

### Session 12: Annotation, Titles, Captions, and Narrative Flow

**Teaching objective:** Teach students how text guides interpretation.

**Title ladder:**

| Type | Example |
|---|---|
| Descriptive | Monthly Revenue |
| Analytical | Revenue recovered after April campaign |
| Action-oriented | Continue campaign in South; diagnose Central response |

**Right way:** Use concise, evidence-linked titles and annotations.

**Wrong way:** Use generic variable-name titles.

---

### Session 13: Common Pitfalls in Data Storytelling

**Teaching objective:** Build responsible visual communication habits.

**Pitfalls to highlight:**

- truncated axes
- chart junk
- 3D distortion
- poor color use
- missing context
- overclaiming
- weak sequencing

**Right way:** Make charts truthful, clear, and useful for action.

**Wrong way:** Use persuasive visuals that lead the audience in the wrong direction.

---

### Session 14: Strategy Communication through Visual Stories

**Teaching objective:** Teach visuals for strategic choices and trade-offs.

**Right way:** Show options, trade-offs, risks, and recommended direction.

**Wrong way:** Recommend the largest market without considering growth, competition, or capability.

**Misinterpretation:** Largest opportunity always means best opportunity.

**Clarification:** Strategic decisions require trade-off thinking.

---

### Session 15: Integrated Storytelling with Data Workshop

**Teaching objective:** Bring the full course together.

**Story arc:**

```text
Problem → Data → Chart → Insight → Implication → Recommendation → Limitation
```

**Right way:** Select only visuals that support the decision story.

**Wrong way:** Present every chart created during analysis.

**Activity:** Ask each group to remove one chart. If the story improves, the chart was unnecessary.

---

### Session 16: Healthcare Data Visualization

**Teaching objective:** Apply data storytelling to healthcare dashboards.

**Right way:** Interpret healthcare metrics with volume, acuity, safety, and operational context.

**Wrong way:** Rank departments by one metric and make immediate staffing recommendations.

**Misinterpretation:** Higher waiting time always means poorer care.

**Clarification:** Healthcare data needs careful context because metrics represent patient experience and safety.

## 10. Final Integrated Workshop Guide

The final workshop should require students to build a complete visual story.

### Suggested Workflow

1. Select dataset.
2. Define business problem.
3. Identify audience.
4. Select appropriate chart.
5. Build visual evidence.
6. Write insight.
7. Add annotation.
8. Write recommendation.
9. Mention limitation.
10. Export final story.

### Model Final Story Format

```text
Business problem:
The management team needs to identify the most important performance issue.

Visual evidence:
The ranked chart shows that Region Central is the weakest performer while West leads overall sales.

Insight:
The performance gap is not uniform across regions, suggesting that a targeted diagnostic is better than a broad intervention.

Recommendation:
Prioritize a focused review of Central region operations, pricing, and customer engagement before reallocating budget.

Limitation:
The chart does not include competitor activity, local demand, or channel-level execution data.
```

## 11. Common Student Mistakes and Corrections

| Mistake | Correction |
|---|---|
| Describing the chart without insight | Ask: “Why should a manager care?” |
| Choosing chart type randomly | Ask: “What is the business question?” |
| Claiming causation from correlation | Ask: “What evidence proves causality?” |
| Overusing color | Ask: “Where should the audience look first?” |
| Using generic titles | Ask students to write an action-oriented title. |
| Ignoring limitations | Require one caution statement in every answer. |
| Presenting too many charts | Ask which chart can be removed without weakening the story. |

## 15. Teaching Philosophy

This course should help students develop judgement. A technically correct chart is not enough. Students should learn to ask:

- Is this visual honest?
- Is it clear?
- Does it answer the decision question?
- Does it guide attention?
- Does it support a recommendation?
- Does it acknowledge uncertainty?

The final goal is to help students communicate analytics in a way that improves managerial decision-making.

---

## 16. Instructor Closing Line

A strong visual story does not merely show data. It helps people see what matters, understand why it matters, and decide what to do next.
